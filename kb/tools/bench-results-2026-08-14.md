## Q2: Fixed admission max + grace periods

**Question:** Under the new fixed admission period rule, what is the maximum initial admission period for an F-1 student, and what grace periods apply?

### Engine A

Under the new rule, an F-1 student is admitted for a fixed period equal to the length of their program as listed on the Form I-20, up to a maximum of 4 years.

Here's how the associated time periods work:

**Arrival window:** A student may enter up to 30 days before the program start date listed on the I-20. These days do **not** count against the 4-year maximum.

**Departure grace period:** After the program end date (or the end of authorized OPT/STEM OPT), the student gets an additional 30 days to depart the U.S. or take steps to maintain lawful status. This 30-day period also does **not** count toward the 4-year maximum.

A few important points:
- The 4-year clock starts from the **program start date**, not the date of physical entry — so the early-arrival window and the departure period are both excluded from the calculation.
- If a student finishes their program early, the DSO should shorten the date in SEVIS, and the 30-day departure clock runs from that new completion date.
- Certain categories have different limits — for example, English language training programs are capped at 24 months, and public high school study is limited to an aggregate of 12 months.
- Students needing more time (e.g., a program longer than 4 years, or to pursue OPT/STEM OPT) generally must file an extension of stay (EOS) with USCIS before their current admission period expires, or depart and re-enter.

Note that this reflects the fixed-admission-period framework replacing the older "duration of status" model. If you'd like, I can walk through how these periods interact with OPT or program transfers.

### Engine B

Under the final rule (effective September 15, 2026 — not yet in effect as of today), an F-1 student is admitted for a fixed period covering the length of the program listed on the Form I-20, not to exceed **4 years**, plus a **30-day grace period for departure** (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i), (v)).

Key points on how the periods are calculated:

- **The 4-year clock runs from the program start date** on the I-20 — the actual start of the program of study, whether first day of classes or research. It does not run from the date of admission.
- **The 30-day pre-program arrival window does not count** toward the 4-year maximum. F students may still enter up to 30 days before the program start date, and those days are excluded from the calculation.
- **The 30-day post-completion grace period also does not count** toward the 4-year maximum, which avoids students coming up 30 days short of a 4-year program.
- **Early completion:** if the student finishes the program before the program end date on the I-20, the DSO should shorten the end date in SEVIS, and the student (and eligible dependents) has 30 days *from the actual end of study or training* to depart or otherwise seek to maintain lawful status — regardless of a later date on the I-94 (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(v)).
- **Extensions:** students needing more time (program completion, post-completion OPT, STEM OPT, or a new program) must file an EOS with USCIS; approved extensions run to the new end date (e.g., the EAD end date for OPT) plus a fresh 30-day grace period (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5), (f)(7)).

Note the transition rules differ: students properly maintaining status in D/S on September 15, 2026 remain under D/S until their program end date or 4 years from the effective date, whichever comes first, and F students in that cohort get the 60-day departure period from their previous admission, not 30 days (new 8 CFR 214.1(m)).

Sources: Final rule, "Establishing a Fixed Time Period of Admission" (FR Doc. 2026-14439); new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i), (f)(5)(v), (f)(7); new 8 CFR 214.1(m).

## Q3: Transition: latest stay without EOS

**Question:** For an F-1 student admitted for duration of status before the new rule takes effect, what is the latest date they can remain without filing for an extension of stay?

### Engine A

Good question — this gets at the transition provisions in the new rule. Here's how it works for an F-1 student who was admitted for duration of status (D/S) and is properly maintaining that status on the rule's effective date (which is 60 days after publication in the Federal Register):

**They can remain without filing an EOS until the earlier of:**

1. **The program end date listed on their Form I-20** (the one that's valid on the rule's effective date), **or**
2. **Four years from the rule's effective date** — whichever comes first.

**Plus a departure allowance:** After that point, F-1 students get an additional 60 days to depart the country (note: J-1s get 30 days).

**A few important caveats:**

- **Travel abroad ends the D/S treatment.** If the student leaves the U.S. during this transition period and is readmitted, they'll receive a Form I-94 with a *fixed* end date, just like any newly admitted F-1 — so the transition benefit no longer applies.
- **If they need more time** — for example, to finish their program, or to pursue post-completion OPT, STEM OPT, or start a new program — they must file an extension of stay (Form I-539) with USCIS before their authorized stay ends.
- **Special OPT provision:** An F-1 student who timely files a Form I-765 for post-completion OPT or a STEM OPT extension on or before a set deadline (244 days after publication) is *not* required to file a separate EOS — approval of the I-765 alone is sufficient (as long as they don't depart the U.S. before filing).

So in short: **the program end date on the I-20, or four years from the effective date, whichever is earlier, plus 60 days to depart.**

### Engine B

An F-1 student in the U.S. on D/S when the rule takes effect may remain without filing an EOS until **the program end date on their current Form I-20 or 4 years after the rule's effective date, whichever comes first**, plus the 60-day departure period carried over from their previous D/S admission (new 8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)). Note that as of today the rule is final but not yet in effect — it takes effect 60 days after publication, in mid-September 2026.

Key conditions and caveats:

- **Continued enrollment required.** The transition protection applies only if the student continues the studies listed on the I-20; no I-94 correction is needed during the 4-year transition period (8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)).
- **Grace period.** Transition students keep the 60-day departure period "as provided in their previous admission." (Students admitted after the effective date get the new, shorter 30-day period instead.)
- **Travel resets the clock.** If the student departs during the transition period, they will be admitted on return with a fixed-date I-94 based on the I-20 end date plus 30 days, and any further time requires an EOS (8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)(ii), 214.1(a)(4)).
- **OPT/STEM OPT exception.** A D/S F-1 who timely files Form I-765 for post-completion OPT or a STEM OPT extension within six months of the effective date (the rule states on or before March 18, 2027) does not need to file an EOS — only the I-765. If approved, F-1 status runs until the EAD expiration plus 60 days (8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)(i), (m)(2)(i)).
- **Beyond the cap.** To stay past the program end date or the 4-year maximum, the student must timely file Form I-539 with USCIS or depart and seek readmission; a timely-filed EOS confers a period of authorized stay with continued study, and CPT/on-campus/hardship employment auto-extends up to 240 days (unless the EOS was filed during the grace period, in which case no employment until approval).

One practical flag: the preamble is internally inconsistent on the exact transition dates (one passage references September 15, 2026; the six-month I-765 window runs to March 18, 2027, implying a September 18 effective date). Confirm the operative effective date from the rule's DATES section or SEVP implementation guidance before advising on hard deadlines.

## Q4: English language training cap

**Question:** What is the maximum admission period for an F-1 student in an English language training program under the new rule?

### Engine A

Under the new rule, F-1 students enrolled in English language training (ELT) programs are limited to an aggregate maximum of **24 months** of study. On top of that, they get an additional 30-day period to prepare for departure or otherwise seek to maintain lawful status.

A few important points about how this works:

- **It's an aggregate lifetime limit.** The 24 months is calculated cumulatively from the student's program start date, and it's applied prospectively (programs completed before the rule's effective date won't count toward the limit).
- **Breaks and vacations count.** The 24 months includes school breaks and annual vacations. If a student leaves the U.S. and returns to resume studies, they don't get additional time to make up for time spent abroad.
- **Extenuating circumstances don't extend it.** Even situations like illness or emergencies won't push the limit past 24 months.
- **You can still study English in another status.** A student admitted for a non-ELT program (like a degree program) isn't prevented from taking English coursework during that stay—the 24-month cap specifically targets standalone ELT programs.

The rationale behind this was to curb cases where students remained in the U.S. for very long periods—sometimes many years—through continuous language study without clear academic progress.

### Engine B

Under the final rule, an F-1 student whose course of study is an English language training (ELT) program is limited to a **maximum admission period of 24 months, plus an additional 30-day period** to depart the United States or otherwise seek to maintain lawful status (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(A)).

Key points for advising:

- **It's an aggregate lifetime limit, applied prospectively.** The 24 months is calculated as an aggregate from the program start date and includes school breaks and annual vacations. ELT programs completed *before* the rule's effective date do not count toward the limit (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(ii)(C); 91 FR 45039).
- **No credit for time abroad.** Students may depart and reenter (e.g., for a break), but do not receive additional admission time for time spent outside the U.S., and extenuating circumstances (illness, disability, emergencies) do not extend the 24-month cap for standalone ELT students (91 FR 45039–45040).
- **Pathway programs are treated differently.** A student admitted to a degree program that integrates a pathway program at the same institution is granted admission covering both, up to the general 4-year maximum — the ELT-specific 24-month cap does not truncate that. Note that DSOs remain prohibited from issuing Forms I-20 based on conditional admission (8 CFR 214.3(k)).
- **Transition period.** F-1 ELT students admitted for D/S and present in the U.S. on the rule's effective date may continue their program until the program end date, not to exceed 4 years (new 8 CFR 214.1(m)).

After exhausting the 24 months, a nonimmigrant may still take English classes incidental to another authorized admission (e.g., an F-1 admitted for a degree program), but cannot be admitted for further standalone ELT study.

Sources: 91 FR 45324 (final rule), new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(A), 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(ii)(C), 8 CFR 214.1(m), 8 CFR 214.3(k)

## Q5: Public high school limit

**Question:** How long may an F-1 student attend a U.S. public high school?

### Engine A

An F-1 student is limited to a total of 12 months of study at any U.S. public high school. This is a cumulative (aggregate) cap, and it counts across all public high schools combined — the clock doesn't reset if the student changes schools. Under current guidance, this 12-month period also includes school breaks and annual vacations.

There's an important financial condition too: the student must reimburse the local school district for the full, unsubsidized per-capita cost of the education for the entire period of attendance, and provide proof of that payment.

A few related points worth keeping in mind:
- F-1 students **cannot** attend a public elementary or middle school, or a publicly funded adult education program.
- M-1 students are **not** subject to the same 12-month public high school limit.

### Engine B

An F-1 student is limited to an aggregate of 12 months of study at any public high school(s), and this cap is statutory (INA § 214(m); 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)). The USCIS Policy Manual confirms F-1 students "are limited to a cumulative period of 12 months to attend a public high school" (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2).

Key points for advising:

- **The 12 months are cumulative** across any public high school(s), and under the 2026 duration-of-status final rule, the 12-month aggregate explicitly includes school breaks and annual vacations. The final rule also clarifies that "public high school" includes charter schools or other similar taxpayer-funded schools (91 FR 45103; new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(C)).
- **Cost reimbursement is required.** The student must show proof of reimbursing the local educational agency for the full, unsubsidized per capita cost of education for the period of attendance (INA § 214(m); USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2).
- **Fixed period of admission.** F-1 public high school students are typically admitted for a fixed period rather than D/S; a student who wants to remain beyond that period (e.g., to transfer or begin post-secondary study) must file Form I-539 for an extension of stay (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2).
- **Contrast with private schools:** the 12-month cap does not apply to approved private academic high schools (governed by 8 CFR 214.2(f)(6)(i)(E)), though those students remain subject to the fixed-period admission framework, including the general 4-year maximum under the final rule (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(7)(vi)). Note also that F-1 students may not attend public elementary or middle schools at all.

Sources: INA § 214(m); 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i) and new (f)(5)(i)(C); 91 FR 45103 (final rule); USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2.

## Q6: D/S student travel + re-entry

**Question:** If an F-1 student admitted under duration of status travels abroad and re-enters after September 15, 2026, how are they admitted?

### Engine A

They will be admitted for a **fixed period of admission**, not duration of status. On any admission on or after the September 15, 2026 effective date, the F-1 is admitted for the period necessary to complete the course of study on the Form I-20, not to exceed 4 years, plus the 30-day departure period; they may also be admitted up to 30 days before the program start date, and neither the pre-start days nor the 30-day departure period counts against the 4-year maximum (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i), as added by 91 FR 45124, effective Sept. 15, 2026). Shorter caps apply to certain populations — 24 months aggregate for English language training students and 12 months aggregate for public high school students (8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(A), (C)).

Practical consequences for a student who was in the D/S transition cohort:

- **The transition treatment ends on departure.** A student properly maintaining status on September 15, 2026 who never travels remains authorized under the transition rule (up to 4 years from that date). Once they leave and are readmitted, they hold a fixed-date I-94 tied to their program end date.
- **OPT/STEM OPT filings change.** An F-1 in the transition cohort who departs *before* filing for post-completion OPT or STEM OPT and is readmitted with a fixed admission period loses the I-539 exemption — they must file both the Form I-765 and a Form I-539 extension of stay (8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)(ii)).
- **Time needed beyond the fixed admission** — program delays, a new program, OPT beyond the I-94 date — requires an extension of stay filed with USCIS under 8 CFR 214.1(c)(2), not just a DSO program extension in SEVIS.
- Note also that each new admission resets the fixed period; DHS gave the K-12 example that a traveling student gets a new four-year maximum on each readmission (91 FR 45324 preamble discussion of K-12 students).

F-2 dependents' authorized stay cannot exceed the principal's (8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)(E)).

Sources: 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i) and 214.1(m)(1) as amended by the D/S final rule (published July 17, 2026, effective Sept. 15, 2026); final rule preamble (Federal Register doc. 2026-14439); NAFSA regulatory update (July 16, 2026).

Instead, they get a **fixed period of admission**, just like any newly admitted F-1 student.

## Q7: OPT timely-filing transition deadline

**Question:** Under the new rule's transition provisions, what is the deadline connected to OPT applications and the rule's publication date?

### Engine A

Under the transition provisions, F-1 students who timely file their employment authorization application (Form I-765) for post-completion OPT or a STEM OPT extension **on or before the date that falls 244 days after the rule's publication in the Federal Register** are not required to file a separate extension of stay (EOS) application, Form I-539. Only the employment authorization application would be needed.

A few related points worth noting:

- If that OPT/STEM OPT application is approved, the student is authorized to stay in F status until the EAD's expiration date plus 60 days.
- If the application is denied, the student can generally remain until the program end date on their I-20 (valid as of 60 days after publication) plus 60 days, provided they keep pursuing a full course of study and otherwise maintain F-1 status.
- DHS also reserves the ability to extend this filing exemption in six-month increments through notices published in the Federal Register.

Do note that there's a separate 60-day reference point in some of the transition language tied to pending applications and travel scenarios, so the exact 244-day figure specifically applies to the exemption from the EOS filing requirement.

### Engine B

The key deadline is **March 18, 2027**: F-1 students who were admitted for duration of status (D/S) and who timely file Form I-765 for post-completion OPT or a STEM OPT extension **on or before March 18, 2027** are exempt from the new requirement to file an extension of stay (EOS) application (Form I-539) — approval of the I-765 alone suffices (new 8 CFR 214.1(m)(1)(i); 91 FR 45324, final rule preamble).

## Q8: OPT filing window

**Question:** When can an F-1 student file for post-completion OPT, and what is the SEVIS recommendation rule?

### Engine A

An F-1 student may apply for post-completion OPT no earlier than 90 days before the program end date and no later than 60 days after the program end date — a 150-day filing window. Within that window, the Form I-765 must be properly filed with USCIS no more than 30 days after the DSO enters the OPT recommendation into SEVIS (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2; 8 CFR 214.2(f)(11)(i)(B)(2)).

A few related points advisers should keep in mind:

- The DSO's recommendation must be entered in SEVIS and annotated on the Form I-20 before the student files the I-765 (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2).
- The student may not request an employment start date more than 60 days after the program end date, and all OPT must be completed within 14 months of program completion (8 CFR 214.2(f)(11)(i)(B)).
- Employment may not begin until the start date on the EAD; authorization begins on the date the DSO requested or the date USCIS adjudicates the I-765, whichever is later.

One timing note in light of the 2026 duration-of-status final rule (91 FR 45034 et seq.): students who timely file for post-completion OPT or STEM OPT within six months of that rule's effective date need only file the Form I-765 — no separate extension-of-stay (Form I-539) application is required. For students filing after that transition window, whether an EOS is also needed depends on the program end date on the I-20; a student engaging in OPT after completion may need to file EOS concurrently with the I-765, and DHS recommends concurrent filing so the cases are adjudicated together.

Sources: 8 CFR 214.2(f)(11)(i)(B); USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2 (Student Employment); 2026 duration-of-status final rule (FR Doc. 2026-14439).

## Q9: Post-completion grace period

**Question:** What grace period does an F-1 student have after completing their program, and does the new rule change it?

### Engine A

Great question — this is an important distinction right now because there are two frameworks at play.

**Current/existing rule:** F-1 students who complete their course of study and any authorized practical training get a **60-day grace period** to prepare to depart the U.S., transfer to another SEVP-certified school, change education level, or file to change to another status. During this window, the student is considered to be maintaining status.

**The new rule (final rule):** This shortens the departure period from **60 days to 30 days**. So after completing a program or an authorized period of post-completion OPT/STEM OPT, the student would have 30 days to either depart the U.S. or otherwise take steps to maintain lawful status — such as timely filing an extension of stay (EOS) or a change of status application.

A few related points worth noting under the new framework:

- The 30-day departure period will be reflected on the student's Form I-94.
- If a student **ends their study or training early** (before their listed program end date), they must depart or take action to maintain status within 30 days of that new end date.
- The start date requested for post-completion OPT was correspondingly updated so a student can't request a start date more than 30 days (rather than 60) after their program end date.
- Students who withdraw from classes with DSO approval still have a separate **15-day** departure period. Those who fail to maintain a full course of study without DSO approval get no additional departure time.

**A key caution:** If a student files an EOS during that 30-day departure grace period (rather than before their admission period expires), they generally will **not** get an automatic extension of employment authorization and must wait for approval before working.

### Engine B

Under the new fixed-admission final rule (91 FR 45324, effective September 15, 2026), the F-1 post-completion grace period is **30 days** — cut in half from the 60 days allowed under the prior D/S regulations. Within 30 days of the program end date (or the actual end of study or training, if earlier), the student and any eligible dependents must depart the United States or take steps to maintain lawful status, such as timely filing an extension of stay (EOS) or a change of status. *See* new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(v).

Key points advisers should note:

- **Early completion:** If the student finishes before the I-20 program end date, the 30 days run from the actual end of study — not from the I-94 admission end date. The DSO should shorten the program end date in SEVIS (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(v)).
- **The 30 days don't count against the 4-year cap:** The grace period, like the 30-day pre-program arrival window, is excluded from the maximum period of admission calculation (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i)).
- **Conforming OPT changes:** The post-completion OPT filing window and the latest requestable OPT start date are both now 30 days after the program end date, not 60 (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(11)(i)(B)(2) and (f)(11)(i)(D) — the latter was a technical fix made in the final rule after being overlooked in the NPRM).
- **Employment caution:** An EOS filed during the 30-day grace period is still "timely," but the student gets **no automatic extension of employment authorization** (on-campus, CPT, or severe economic hardship) — they may only continue studying and must wait for EOS approval before working. Students should file before entering the grace period to preserve the 240-day automatic extension (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(viii)).
- **Other departure periods unchanged:** 15 days for a DSO-authorized withdrawal; no grace period for status violations or an EOS denial, which requires immediate departure (new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(v)).
- **Transition:** Students maintaining D/S on September 15, 2026 whose program or OPT has already ended as of the effective date retain the remainder of their 60-day departure period under the transition provisions (new 8 CFR 214.1(m)).

Sources: 91 FR 45324 (final rule); new 8 CFR 214.2(f)(5)(i), (f)(5)(v), (f)(5)(viii), (f)(11)(i)(B)(2), (f)(11)(i)(D); new 8 CFR 214.1(m).

## Q10: Full course of study (UG)

**Question:** What is a full course of study for an F-1 undergraduate at a college or university?

### Engine A

For an F-1 undergraduate at a college or university, a full course of study is at least 12 semester or quarter hours of instruction per academic term (at institutions using standard semester, trimester, or quarter hour systems), provided the school charges students enrolled for 12 hours full-time tuition or considers them full-time for other administrative purposes — or its equivalent as determined by SEVP during school certification (8 CFR 214.2(f)(6)(i)(B)).

Key qualifiers:

- **Final-term exception:** A student may drop below 12 hours if they will complete the course of study during the current term with fewer semester or quarter hours.
- **Online/distance limits:** Only one online or distance education class, or three credits (or the equivalent), per academic session may count toward the full course of study if the class does not require physical attendance for any purpose integral to its completion (8 CFR 214.2(f)(6)(i)(G)). If only one course remains to finish the program, it cannot be taken online; it must have a physical presence requirement.
- The DSO must have certified the program as a full course of study, and the study must lead to a specific educational or professional objective.

Note that other reduced course load authorizations (academic difficulty, medical) under 8 CFR 214.2(f)(6)(iii) exist but weren't covered in the material here — consult that paragraph directly for those.

Sources: 8 CFR 214.2(f)(6)(i)(B), (f)(6)(i)(G); USCIS Policy Manual (Students section, full course of study).

### Engine B

For an F-1 undergraduate at a college or university, a full course of study generally means at least 12 semester or quarter hours of instruction per academic term (for schools using standard semester, trimester, or quarter systems). A key condition is that the school must treat 12 hours as full-time — meaning students enrolled at that level are charged full-time tuition or are otherwise considered full-time for administrative purposes. SEVP may recognize an equivalent standard through the school certification process.

There's one common exception: a student in their final term who needs fewer than 12 hours to finish their program may take a lighter load and still be considered in status, as long as the DSO has approved it.

A few related points worth keeping in mind:
- **Online/distance courses:** Only one class or up to three credits per term may count toward the 12-hour requirement if the class is fully online. And a student in their last course cannot complete it online.
- **Reduced course loads:** Any drop below full-time must be authorized by the DSO in advance (for reasons like academic difficulty, medical conditions, or a final term). Dropping below without approval puts the student out of status.
- **On-campus employment** tied to a scholarship, fellowship, or assistantship counts as part of the academic program.

## Q12: Transfer while on OPT

**Question:** What happens to an F-1 student's OPT if they transfer to another school?

### Engine A

OPT — including a STEM OPT extension — is automatically terminated when an F-1 student transfers to another school. The effective termination date is the transfer release date in SEVIS, so the student's work authorization ends on that date even if the EAD card itself shows a later expiration (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2, F-1 practical training chapter).

For the same reason, OPT also terminates automatically if the student begins a new degree level at the same school (effective on the change-of-program date), changes to another nonimmigrant status, fails to maintain F-1 status, or is enrolled at a school that closes.

Adviser-facing takeaway: when setting a release date for a student currently on OPT or STEM OPT, make sure the student understands that employment authorization ends on that release date — a mid-OPT transfer forfeits the remaining authorized period. The student would only regain OPT eligibility by qualifying anew (e.g., 12 additional months of OPT after moving to a higher educational level).

Sources: USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2 (F-1 Optional Practical Training, "General"); see also 8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)–(11).

### Engine B

When an F-1 student transfers to another SEVP-certified school, their OPT authorization is automatically terminated. This applies to all types of OPT, including STEM OPT.

The termination is triggered by the school transfer itself. The same automatic termination also happens if the student begins a new program at a higher educational level, changes to a different nonimmigrant status (such as H-1B), fails to maintain F-1 status, or is enrolled in a school that closes.

For a transfer, the effective termination date is the release date recorded in SEVIS.

A couple of practical points worth keeping in mind:
- A student who wants to resume full-time studies in a new program after post-completion OPT needs to go through the standard transfer procedure.
- If the student is on post-completion or STEM OPT, they must be able to begin or resume classes within 5 months of transferring out of the school that recommended the OPT, or by the date the OPT authorization ends—whichever comes first.

So the key takeaway: initiating a transfer ends the OPT, so timing matters if the student still intends to use remaining training time or start a new program smoothly.

## Q13: CPT integral-to-curriculum scrutiny (new)

**Question:** Can I authorize CPT for a student's internship if the internship is related to their major but not required for their degree? Anything new I should know?

### Engine A

Good question — this gets at the core definition of CPT.

Under the F-1 rules, CPT must be an **integral part of an established curriculum**. The regulatory definition covers alternative work/study, an internship, cooperative education, or any other type of **required internship or practicum** offered by sponsoring employers through cooperative agreements with the school. So the key isn't just that the work relates to the student's major — the training needs to be tied into the curriculum itself.

There are generally two ways an internship qualifies:

1. **The internship is required for the degree** (e.g., a mandatory practicum or co-op built into the program), or
2. **The student earns academic credit for it and it's part of an established course** in the curriculum. In this case the internship, while not required of every student in the program, functions as an integral curricular component through the course requirement.

If the internship is *neither* required for the degree *nor* connected to a course or credit that is part of the established curriculum — it's just related work in the field — that typically would **not** qualify as CPT. In that scenario OPT (pre-completion) may be the more appropriate route.

A few reminders when you do authorize:
- The student must have completed one full academic year and be enrolled full-time (with the grad-program exception for programs requiring immediate CPT).
- Authorize it in SEVIS with employer, location, dates, and full-time/part-time, and give the student the endorsed I-20 **before** they start.
- Watch the 12-months-full-time-CPT threshold, which eliminates OPT eligibility at that level.

**Anything new?** A proposed DHS rule would replace duration-of-status with fixed admission periods and an extension-of-stay (EOS) framework. Notably, it clarifies that if a student timely files an EOS before their I-94 expires, CPT can be "automatically extended" for up to 240 days while the EOS is pending. But if the EOS is filed during the 30-day departure grace period, CPT is **not** auto-extended — the student must wait for approval before working. Importantly, this proposal does **not** change CPT eligibility or the authorization process itself (the DSO's role stays the same), and it did not adopt suggestions like eliminating Day-1 CPT. Since it's still a proposed rule, keep an eye on whether/how it's finalized.

Bottom line: relatedness to the major alone isn't enough — you need that curricular hook. Happy to help you think through a specific scenario if you'd like!

### Engine B

Not on the basis of "related to the major" alone. Relation to the major is necessary but not sufficient — the regulation requires that CPT be "an integral part of an established curriculum," defined as alternative work/study, internship, cooperative education, or any other type of **required** internship or practicum offered by sponsoring employers through cooperative agreements with the school (8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(i); USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2). An offer letter plus subject-matter relevance never establishes eligibility by itself. The question is whether the training has a genuine curricular hook: a for-credit internship or practicum course, degree-audit or catalog language establishing it as part of the program, and a cooperative agreement or documented employer relationship.

Whether a non-mandatory internship can qualify depends on how your institution's curriculum formally incorporates it. Schools have long treated for-credit internship courses and co-op structures as qualifying even where the specific internship isn't mandatory for every student in the degree. But if the experience is neither required nor otherwise formally recognized as part of the academic program — i.e., it exists solely to enable employment — it cannot be authorized as CPT.

**What's new — and it matters here:**

1. **SEVP Broadcast Message 2608-01 (August 12, 2026)** announces increased scrutiny of CPT authorizations that are not "an integral part of an established curriculum." SEVP says it may request documentation from DSOs showing the CPT is "a core and essential part of the student's studies, without which, they would be unable to complete their degree." The broadcast's requested standard — authorize only if the training's "absence would make the attainment of a degree an impossibility" and it is "required for all students pursuing that degree" — is stricter than the regulatory text, and the broadcast itself disclaims the force of law; it does not amend 8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(i). But it does describe how SEVP intends to scrutinize files. Practically: a fact pattern like yours (related but not required) is exactly the category SEVP is signaling it will probe, and your file needs to document the curricular connection thoroughly.

2. **Personal exposure.** The broadcast expressly frames the DSO's I-20 signature as a certification under penalty of perjury and warns that improper CPT authorization may subject both the institution and the individual DSO to administrative or legal consequences, up to withdrawal of SEVP certification (SEVP BCM 2608-01).

3. **SEVP's requested actions** include reviewing existing CPT authorizations against current academic requirements across all fields of study — not just gating new requests — and maintaining records supporting curricular necessity for each authorization.

Note also that the recent duration-of-status final rule made no substantive changes to CPT eligibility or the authorization process; it affects employment eligibility during a pending extension-of-stay, not the DSO's CPT determination (91 FR 45035–45036).

If you're weighing a specific borderline case, the broadcast directs DSOs to their SEVP field representative — a reasonable step before authorizing under this fact pattern.

Sources: 8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(i); USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2 (Practical Training); SEVP Broadcast Message 2608-01 (Aug. 12, 2026); 91 FR 45035–45036.

## Q14: Public charge post-9/18 (new)

**Question:** A student asks whether enrolling in SNAP or Medicaid could affect their immigration status. What should I tell them after this fall?

### Engine A

The advice changes on September 18, 2026. DHS published a final rule on July 20, 2026 (91 FR 45324) that rescinds the 2022 Public Charge Rule in its entirety, effective September 18, 2026 — so what you tell the student depends on when the benefit is received or applied for.

**Before September 18, 2026 (2022 rule, 87 FR 55472):** Medicaid (non-institutional) and SNAP explicitly do not count for public charge. Only public cash assistance for income maintenance (SSI, TANF, state/local General Assistance) and long-term institutionalization at government expense are considered.

**On or after September 18, 2026 (2026 final rule):** receipt of *any* means-tested public benefit — federal, state, tribal, territorial, or local — may be considered, and Medicaid and SNAP are squarely means-tested. Two changes worth flagging expressly:

- **Applying counts, not just receiving.** DHS confirmed that application, approval, or certification to receive a means-tested benefit may be considered — but only for applications made on or after the effective date.
- **No carve-outs.** DHS declined to exclude health-related or public-health benefits, and declined to codify a definition of "means-tested public benefit." The old codified framework at 8 CFR 212.20–212.23 is removed; officers will decide case by case under USCIS subregulatory guidance.

**Where the exposure actually arises:** public charge is not assessed for maintaining F-1 status inside the U.S. The student would face it at (1) visa issuance/renewal at a consulate, (2) each admission at a port of entry (including reentry after travel), and (3) any later adjustment of status. F and J nonimmigrants are covered — DHS explicitly declined to exempt them — though it noted a CBP inadmissibility finding for a returning student is "unlikely… barring additional information or changed circumstances."

**Framing for the student:** consideration is not disqualification. Determinations remain discretionary and totality-based, and public charge denials have historically been extremely rare (DHS cites 0.0087% of I-485 applicants over five years). But the safe-harbor advice that "SNAP and Medicaid can't affect you" is only accurate for benefits received before September 18, 2026, and should not be repeated after that without the date qualifier. Watch for the USCIS subregulatory guidance due by the effective date before advising on how specific benefits will be weighed. For a student with concrete plans to travel or adjust status, this is a place where referral to immigration counsel is genuinely warranted.

Sources: 91 FR 45324 (final rule, published July 20, 2026; effective Sept. 18, 2026); 87 FR 55472 (2022 rule, in force for benefits received before Sept. 18, 2026); 8 CFR 212.20–212.23 (removed effective Sept. 18, 2026).

### Engine B

Good question — this comes up a lot, so let me break it down.

**The key distinction is between which benefits actually count toward a public charge determination and which do not.**

For most immigration purposes, the benefits that *are* considered are quite narrow:
- Public cash assistance for income maintenance (things like SSI, TANF cash aid, or comparable state/local cash income-maintenance programs)
- Long-term institutionalization at government expense (e.g., a nursing facility or mental health institution paid for by the government)

**SNAP (food assistance) and most Medicaid are NOT counted.** Specifically:
- **SNAP** is expressly listed among benefits that are *not* considered in a public charge inadmissibility determination.
- **Medicaid** is generally not counted either — the *only* Medicaid-related exception is long-term institutional care (nursing home/mental institution). Regular Medicaid coverage, home- and community-based services, and CHIP are all excluded from consideration.

A couple of practical points worth passing along:

1. **F-1/J-1 students generally aren't subject to public charge in their nonimmigrant status.** Public charge inadmissibility is primarily relevant at moments like adjusting to permanent resident status (green card). And benefits received while someone is in a status *exempt* from public charge are typically not counted.

2. **Eligibility is a separate question from immigration consequences.** Many nonimmigrants (including most F-1 students) aren't actually eligible for these programs to begin with, so it's worth having the student confirm their eligibility with the benefit-granting agency before assuming they can enroll.

**A cautious bottom line for the student:** Enrolling in SNAP or ordinary Medicaid, on its own, does not affect their status or a future green card determination under current rules. But because policies in this area have shifted historically, it's wise to keep records and, for anything tied to a future immigration filing, consult an immigration attorney.

I'd steer clear of giving them a definitive legal opinion — point them to official USCIS guidance and, if they're planning a status change, an immigration attorney. Happy to help you frame that message for them if you'd like!
