# Public Charge — 2026 Final Rule (SUPERSEDES the 2022 rule)

**Status: FINAL RULE. Published July 20, 2026 (91 FR 45324). Effective September 18, 2026.**
This is not a proposal. The NPRM was published November 19, 2025 (90 FR 52168); DHS finalized it **as proposed** on July 20, 2026. Any prior source describing the 2022 Public Charge Rule (87 FR 55472) describes law that is **rescinded effective September 18, 2026**.

## What the rule does

DHS rescinds the 2022 Public Charge Ground of Inadmissibility rule in its entirety. All of 8 CFR 212.20–212.23 is removed: the applicability section, all definitions (including "likely at any time to become a public charge" and "public cash assistance for income maintenance"), the determination framework, and the codified list of exemptions and waivers. No new regulatory framework replaces them. USCIS will issue subregulatory guidance that "informs, but does not prescribe" officer decisions. Officers decide case by case in the totality of the circumstances.

## The substantive change

- **Old standard (2022 rule, applies to benefits received BEFORE September 18, 2026):** only public cash assistance for income maintenance (e.g., SSI, TANF, state/local cash "General Assistance") and long-term institutionalization at government expense counted. Medicaid (non-institutional), SNAP, WIC, CHIP, housing assistance, and the Essential Plan explicitly did NOT count.
- **New standard (benefits received, applied for, or approved ON OR AFTER September 18, 2026):** receipt of **any means-tested public benefit** may be considered — federal, **state, tribal, territorial, and local** programs included. DHS confirmed this expressly in the preamble.
- **Applying counts, not just receiving.** DHS confirmed that an alien's "receipt, as well as application, approval, or certification to receive" any means-tested public benefit will be considered. Under the 2022 rule, merely applying did not count. Now it can — but only applications made on or after September 18, 2026.
- **No definition of "means-tested public benefit."** DHS declined to codify one, stating the term is "generally understood" to mean a government program where eligibility is based on income and/or assets falling below a threshold.
- **No carve-outs.** DHS expressly declined to exclude means-tested benefits related to public health, prenatal care, other health-related issues, or workforce development.

## Whose benefits count

Only benefits received by the alien who is applying for admission or adjustment are considered (with limited exceptions discussed in the preamble). Benefits received by U.S.-citizen family members (e.g., a citizen child's Medicaid) are not formally attributed to the applicant.

## Who it applies to, and when it bites

- Applies to **applications for admission made on or after September 18, 2026** and **adjustment of status applications postmarked or electronically filed on or after September 18, 2026**.
- **F and J visa holders are covered.** DHS explicitly declined a commenter's request to exempt F and J categories: public charge inadmissibility applies to nonimmigrant visa applicants, applicants for admission, and adjustment applicants unless a statutory exemption applies. DHS noted a CBP inadmissibility finding for a returning student at a port of entry is "unlikely… barring additional information or changed circumstances."
- Practical exposure points for an F-1 student: (1) visa issuance/renewal at a consulate (DOS applies its own public charge analysis under the FAM), (2) each admission at a port of entry, including reentry after travel, (3) later adjustment of status (e.g., after OPT/H-1B). Public charge is NOT assessed for maintaining status inside the U.S.; an F-1 in duration of status who never travels and never files for a benefit of admission or adjustment is not being adjudicated.
- Statutory exemptions (refugees, asylees, and other categories exempted by statute) still exist — they come from the INA, not the removed 8 CFR 212.23 list. But the convenient codified regulatory list is gone.

## Public charge bonds (8 CFR 103.6(c)(1), as amended)

- Bonds posted **on or after September 18, 2026** are breached if the bonded alien receives **any** means-tested public benefit (or violates any bond condition). Bonds posted before that date keep the old breach standard (cash assistance / long-term institutionalization).
- Cancellation after the 5-year anniversary now requires the alien to affirmatively file a cancellation request on the USCIS-designated form. DHS removed the provision letting USCIS cancel a bond at any time upon finding the alien unlikely to become a public charge.

## Advising implications (DSO framing)

- The widely repeated advice "Medicaid/SNAP/WIC/Essential Plan don't count for public charge" is **correct only for benefits received before September 18, 2026**. After that date it is stale and should not be repeated without the date qualifier.
- **New York specifics:** the NY Essential Plan (free/low-cost health coverage; F-1 students residing in NY may enroll; income limit 200% FPL as of July 1, 2026) is means-tested — income-capped — and state-administered, so it is within what DHS says officers may consider for enrollment on or after September 18, 2026. Many advising resources still say Essential Plan enrollment cannot affect immigration status; that reflects the rescinded 2022 rule.
- Other income-tested programs open to people regardless of immigration status (so nonimmigrants and their dependents can actually enroll) include: Fair Fares NYC (half-price transit), NYC Care (sliding-scale H+H health care), WIC, Head Start and subsidized child care, HEAP/Cooling Assistance, Lifeline phone/internet discounts, and affordable-housing lotteries (income-banded — and note that *applying* can now be considered).
- Programs that are universal (no income test) remain outside any reading of "means-tested": IDNYC, NYC 3-K/Pre-K for All, NYC universal free school meals, libraries.
- Weight vs. admissibility: consideration is not disqualification. Determinations are discretionary and totality-based, and public charge denials have historically been extremely rare (DHS cites 0.0087% of I-485 applicants over a five-year period, with sustained denials centered on missing/insufficient Form I-864). The near-term change is uncertainty and discretion, not a categorical bar.
- Scholarships, stipends, and assistantships: DHS said this rule "does not regulate universities nor the eligibility for, or amount of, financial aid awards." It did not state that receipt of government-funded, income-tested financial aid is excluded from consideration. Institutional merit aid and assistantships are compensation/private aid, not public benefits.

## Citation hygiene (important for answering questions)

- 2019 Final Rule (Trump-era, 84 FR 41292): vacated/rescinded — historical only.
- 2022 Final Rule (87 FR 55472): in force for benefits received before September 18, 2026; rescinded as of that date.
- 2025 NPRM (90 FR 52168, Nov. 19, 2025): the proposal stage of this rule — never cite as current law.
- **2026 Final Rule (91 FR 45324, July 20, 2026): current law from September 18, 2026.**
- Field guidance: with 8 CFR 212.20–212.23 removed, the operative day-to-day standard will be USCIS subregulatory guidance issued on or before the effective date; check for it before answering post-September questions about how officers weigh specific benefits.
